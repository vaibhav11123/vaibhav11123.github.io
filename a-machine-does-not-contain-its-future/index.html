A Machine Does Not Contain Its Future

A spinning sphere, the Roman world, and the stories we tell about AI.
By Vaibhav Singh  ·  3 min read
Artwork: 19th-c. engraving, public domain, Wikimedia Commons

A bronze sphere turns above a cauldron. Steam escapes through bent tubes, and the sphere spins.

A modern eye sees a steam engine. Then comes the irresistible question: why didn't the Roman world build an industrial revolution around it?

But why are we so sure that was its unfinished job?

Hero of Alexandria described the device in his Pneumatica. Courtney Ann Roby's study challenges the habit of treating it as a failed ancestor of the factory engine. She discusses an interpretation of it as a contribution to philosophical debate about motion.

A machine could be an argument. We look at it and see a business plan that never happened.

If the sphere helped someone think about nature, was that an inferior purpose? Or have we quietly decided that every invention must justify itself through the industry it eventually creates?

The Roman world certainly knew how to put machines to work. At Barbegal, in what is now France, a second-century CE complex used sixteen waterwheels to mill grain. The story is harder to reduce to ancient people failing to recognise useful technology.

None of this explains the absence of a Roman Industrial Revolution. It does suggest a better question than why they failed to become us: what makes a capability meaningful in the world around it?

With AI, the temptation is familiar. We see a demonstration and begin narrating a destiny.

A model writes code. Therefore, a particular future of work is inevitable.

That "therefore" is doing an extraordinary amount of work.

Imagine two organisations using the same capable coding model. One helps junior engineers understand unfamiliar code. The other gives it permission to deploy changes to production. These are hypothetical choices, but their difference matters: the same underlying ability has been placed inside different relationships between people and software.

The capability matters. It does not choose the arrangement.

Of course, technology is not a blank page. Costs, limitations and unequal power constrain what people can choose. A spinning sphere is not a practical power plant, and a model's weaknesses cannot be wished away.

But a constraint is not a complete script.

"We can build this," "this will happen," and "this is a future worth wanting" are different claims. An impressive demonstration cannot do all three jobs.

Looking backwards, it is easy to turn history into a path towards us. Looking forwards, it is easy to mistake our preferred path for a property of the machine.

Hero's sphere spins. It shows a possibility.

What should turn with it is still a question.
